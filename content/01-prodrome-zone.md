# The Prodrome Zone

**Hook:** A small deep-brain crossroads where this companion's migraine theory begins.

## Where it is

In a mid-sagittal brain view, this region sits deep in the center of the brain, above the midbrain and around the third ventricle. The thalamus is the larger central mass; the hypothalamus lies just below and slightly forward of it, above the pituitary stalk; the pineal gland is a small midline structure tucked behind the thalamus, near the back of the third ventricle.

## What it does

Established anatomy: these are three distinct structures, not one medical unit. The thalamus helps relay and filter sensory signals; the hypothalamus helps regulate body state, hormones, temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep, and autonomic activity; the pineal gland produces melatonin, which helps time sleep-wake rhythms.

## The prodrome connection

Established migraine science recognizes that prodrome symptoms can involve changes in body state before migraine onset: sleepiness, food cravings, mood shifts, yawning, light sensitivity, or a sense that “something is coming.” Those symptoms overlap with functions influenced by deep-brain systems, including sensory filtering, circadian timing, and autonomic regulation.

The theory: this cluster is known as the “Prodrome Zone” because the model proposes that migraine begins when an “energy force” enters this deep-brain region at a particular angle. In the hypothesis, different angles pass through or affect different parts of the thalamus, hypothalamus, or pineal area, helping explain why one person’s warning signs may differ from another’s.
